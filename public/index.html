Pick an offense and a defense from all 138 FBS programs. The board shows their real starters. Hand it to a back, keep it with the quarterback, or throw to a receiver, and the simulator plays the snap a thousand times, drawing half the outcomes from what that offense has done on that kind of play this season and half from what that defense has allowed. An AI coordinator can scout both sides and tell you what to call.

Starters are inferred from this season's play-by-play: who carries, who gets targeted, who makes plays. Hover a number for the player. Linemen come from the roster; the public feed has no tackle data, so defenders are ranked by sacks, breakups, picks and forced fumbles.

How it works

Every snap you simulate is a real play. For the call you choose, the simulator collects the offense's plays of that kind this season (and, if you pick a player, that player's plays), narrowed to the same down and distance when there are at least thirty of them. It collects the defense's plays of that kind the same way: what they have actually allowed. Each simulated snap draws from one pool or the other with equal odds, keeps the real yards gained and whether it was a sack, a pick, a fumble or a stuffed run, and converts that into expected points for the down, distance and field position you set. A play that gains four yards on third and three scores better than one that gains eight on third and twelve.

Compare every call runs this for each back, the quarterback keeper, and each receiver with enough targets, and ranks them. The scouting report is straight arithmetic on the same play-by-play. The coordinator is Claude with those three things as tools; it is told not to quote a number it did not get from a run.

What the simulator does not know

The public play-by-play has no tackles, no formations, no pass depth and no coverage, so defensive starters are inferred from impact plays, formations on the board are templates you choose, and the dots in Watch One are an illustration of the sampled outcome, not tracking. Opponent strength is only partly handled: a defense's "allowed" numbers include the quality of the offenses it faced. Early-season samples are small, which is why each result says how many real plays it rests on and how specific the filter could be.
